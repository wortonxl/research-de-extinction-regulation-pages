Resolution Context / Topic: De-extinction biotechnology (e.g., Colossal Biosciences' projects to revive proxy mammoths, thylacines, dodos, dire wolves) operates within an acknowledged domestic and international regulatory vacuum. With no statutes, treaties, or federal agencies holding integrated authority over gene-edited extinct proxies, de facto governance, safety thresholds, and release criteria are dictated by private biotechnology corporations themselves.

Executive Summary & Debate Utility

  • The Inherency / Uniqueness Claim: There is no dedicated statute, international treaty, or federal agency equipped with clear legal jurisdiction over the recreation and wild release of extinct proxy animals. The Endangered Species Act (ESA) cannot classify extinct animals (which lack extant populations or "range"), the Coordinated Framework for Biotechnology splits molecular and welfare rules without lifecycle wild-release oversight, and global biodiversity treaties (CITES, CBD) lack direct mechanisms.
  • The Link / Self-Regulation Claim: Because statutory governance does not exist, governance defaults to private self-regulation. Biotechnology companies like Colossal Biosciences establish their own internal ethics boards, draft their own voluntary standards, and operate according to commercial and pecuniary priorities rather than binding democratic or public-trust mandates.
  • The Impact / Risk Claim: Private self-regulation creates moral hazards, proprietary ownership over revived ecosystems, unchecked corporate discretion over ecological introduction, and permanent genetic changes to wild biomes without accountability or public oversight.

CARD 1: De-Extinction Is Going Forward Unregulated; Corporate Self-Regulation Replaces Government Authority

Author: Hope M. Babcock (Professor of Law, Georgetown University Law Center)
Publication: The Genie is Out of the De-Extinction Bottle: A Problem in Risk Regulation and Regulatory Gaps, Georgetown Law Faculty Publications (SSRN / Environmental Law), Dec 3, 2019
Source URL: https://scholarship.law.georgetown.edu/facpub/2210/
Direct PDF Link: https://scholarship.law.georgetown.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3228&context=facpub

"science appears to be outpacing the ability of government agencies to respond to the legal, moral, and practical questions that these endeavors raise. Existing laws, like the Endangered Species Act ('ESA'), are inadequate to respond to the environmental and public welfare risks these ventures pose. At the same time, the overlapping and conflicting policies governing genetic engineering, upon which de-extinction depends, have created a dysfunctional regulatory commons in which no single agency is responsible for creating, implementing, and enforcing applicable rules. … Thus, the existing regulatory gap will enable de-extinction activities to continue unrestrained, and there is little hope of government action closing this gap any time soon."

"In sum, de-extinction is going forward unregulated. Existing environmental laws do not apply, and any effort to adjust these statutes through amendments or through new legislation will likely fail."

"This article proposes that participants in the field of de-extinction undertake traditional governmental functions, including setting, implementing, and enforcing performance standards, until external regulations are developed. However, self-regulation or private governance may not arise on its own. Therefore, this article suggests that pressure from the application of social norms … might encourage participants in the field of de-extinction to self-regulate."

Source URL: https://arizonastatelawjournal.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Woodward_PUB.pdf

"Multiple regulatory agencies have potential authority over parts of the issue, but no single primary regulator exists. The lack of a regulatory leader increases the opportunity for oversight gaps from regulatory schemes that tend to overlap and contradict each other."

"To reap de-extinction’s benefits and mitigate its potential risks, adequate governance through soft law instruments is necessary."

CARD 8: Corporate Self-Governance in Practice: Internal Voluntary Safeguards Are the Sole Barrier

Source: Colossal Biosciences Corporate Disclosures and Ethics Oversight Manifestos
Publication: Colossal Biosciences Public Portal ("Ethics Oversight & Safeguards" & "How Colossal Prioritizes Ethics"), 2026
Source URLs:
- https://colossal.com/colossal-biosciences-ethics-oversight-safeguards/
- https://colossal.com/how-colossal-biosciences-prioritizes-ethics-in-de-extinction/

In its official governance documentation, Colossal Biosciences defines its oversight structure as resting on: 1. An internal, privately appointed Ethics Advisory Board composed of corporate-selected academics and advisors. 2. Standard lab Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) and baseline USDA Animal Welfare Act registrations designed strictly for commercial lab housing, not wild ecological release. 3. Voluntary commitment to the IUCN Species Survival Commission guidelines.
